## Topic picking
<!-- strange-history's original rules, moved from the code 2026-10-22 -->
Viewers decide in one second whether to swipe, so pick stories whose single most shocking fact can be
said in 8 words or fewer that a stranger understands instantly (e.g. "A dead man won this horse race."),
with a strange moment the Short can open inside of and an answer or twist it can save for the very end.
Rule of the eccentric character: Every Short needs a MAIN CHARACTER: one named person or animal the story follows,
with a real photo or portrait of them. Our Shorts built around one strange person or animal (Jack the baboon, Violet Jessop,
Emperor Norton, Lord Byron and his bear, Hiroo Onoda, Mike the headless chicken, Wojtek the bear) were all pushed by
YouTube, and the named characters brought nearly all our subscribers; the ones about a thing, an event or a
group (a smell, a newspaper hoax, a fake statue, the WW2 Ghost Army) stalled, some under 60 views. Always tell an event
through the eccentric person at its center (the Great Stink through engineer Joseph Bazalgette, the Cardiff Giant through
George Hull who carved it). Choose an event with nobody to follow only when nothing better is left.

## Story rules
<!-- strange-history's original rules, moved from the code 2026-10-22 -->
- First line (research 2026-10-27): the hits open inside the moment as it happens, never with a summary.
  Voggle's Mike (2.7M views): "The axe cut off the rooster's head, but as it dropped into the dirt, the rest of the
  bird got back up". Ours (1.4K): "This chicken lived 18 months without a head."
- How to tell it (learned from the true-story Shorts that hold viewers best; follow it closely):
  Target length: 95-110 spoken words (35-42 seconds). Every word counts; cut every bit of padding.
  hook (scene 1): viewers decide in one second whether to swipe. OPEN INSIDE THE STORY'S STRANGEST MOMENT, told
  as it happens, not as a summary: "A passenger looked out the train window and saw a baboon working the
  signals." / "Halfway through the race, the jockey died, and his horse kept running." Scene 1 must be a
  complete, punchy stand-alone sentence ending with a period or exclamation mark. It shows WHAT is happening
  and holds back WHY, so the viewer needs the answer. Weak: a summary that tells the whole story at once ("A
  railway once hired a baboon.", "Australia's army once lost a war to birds."): once the viewer knows the story,
  nothing is left to stay for. The first sentence: at most 12 words, crystal-clear action understood with no
  context, never a greeting, a setup, a date or a name nobody knows yet. The opening picture already shows the
  subject, so the words add what it can't. If the story is famous, open on its least-known moment. Tell it through
  the main_character ("He...", "This baboon...", their name once they act). Draft 3 first sentences in
  hook_options, each from a different hook family (in-the-moment scene, result first, stakes, contrarian
  claim, before/after), then open scene 1 with the strongest.
  ONE STORY, NOT A LIST OF FACTS: link every beat to the one before with "but", "so" or "you see" (a
  complication or a consequence), never "and then". If the beats could be reordered without breaking
  anything, it is a list: rewrite it.
  scene 2: immediate escalation, complication or stakes ("But the baboon had a very good reason to be
  there." / "If the navy found out, the crew faced treason."), never a date, setup or recap. Zero backstory yet.
  scene 3 and 4: context and backstory arrive ONLY AFTER the curiosity is hooked, brought in with "you see"
  right when it explains something ("You see, the signalman had lost both legs..."), never as a timeline ("In 1852,
  he...", "Japan declared him dead in 1959."). No line starts with a date; a year only when it matters,
  mid-sentence. Names only for people who act in the story.
  Pronunciation for VoiceStudio TTS: write out words phonetically where TTS might stumble: spaced acronyms
  like "H M S" or "U S S", full words like "World War One", "World War Two", and write years out as spoken words
  where needed (e.g. "nineteen-oh-six" instead of "1906") so narration never stumbles or reads dates mechanically.
  escalate: each beat stranger or bigger than the last. Concrete, physical details (what people saw, heard,
  held), told like a friend at a bar, with one or two dry, wry asides that make it fun (never about death or
  suffering).
  payoff LAST: the answer to the hook's question (the reveal or twist) is the final scene or the final two,
  and the Short ends there: no epilogue after it, no "follow for more" (an on-screen subscribe button and the
  pinned comment handle that). Where it fits, the reveal echoes the hook so the ending loops into a rewatch.
  CIRCULAR LOOP ENDING: write the very last sentence so it connects grammatically or conceptually right back into
  scene 1's opening line (e.g. ending on "...all because..." or echoing scene 1's hook subject so the replay begins
  seamlessly before the viewer realizes the video ended). This circular loop drives average percentage viewed over 100%.
  hook_text: 2-4 punchy, high-contrast words highlighting the core anomaly or stakes (e.g. "IMPOSTORS ON A WARSHIP",
  "NOBODY FIRED HIM", "DEAD JOCKEY WINS"), never a generic label.
  Example of the style (a story we already covered; never reuse its wording), one picture per scene:
  ["A passenger looked out the train window", "and saw a baboon working the railway signals.",
  "He complained, so the railway sent officials to investigate.", "But the baboon had a very good reason to be there.",
  "You see, the signalman, James Wide,", "had lost both legs in a fall between two train cars.",
  "So he bought a baboon named Jack", "and taught him the code: so many whistles, that lever.",
  "The officials didn't fire Jack. They gave him an exam.", "Whistle after whistle, he never pulled a wrong lever.",
  "So the railway hired him: twenty cents a day", "and half a bottle of beer every week.",
  "In nine years on the job, he never made a single mistake."]

## Writing for the picture
<!-- strange-history's original rules, moved from the code 2026-10-22 -->
- Write for the picture: every scene must be about something a camera could show (a person,
  place, object, document or crowd), never an abstract line like "this changed everything". If a
  line has nothing to show, rewrite it around a concrete detail.
- Build on the real pictures: each topic's "pictures" lists the real photos and film found for it (#number,
  what it shows; [opener] = strong enough to open the Short). Where a strong picture exists, let the story pass
  through what it shows, and write the hook for an [opener] picture when one fits. The story rules above still
  come first: never bend a fact or the pacing to fit a picture.

## Lessons from our numbers
<!-- strange-history's original rules, moved from the code 2026-10-22 -->
Two weak spots: the first second (Shorts the feed pushed still lost 4-8 of every
10 viewers there) and what follows it: the retention curves of most Shorts dropped hardest 4-12 seconds in
and kept sliding to the end, while the two that held best (Great Stink, Emperor Norton: about 90% of the
video watched) kept their main question open until the last scene and made every beat stranger than the last.

## Opening picture
<!-- strange-history's original rules, moved from the code 2026-10-22 -->
- era_ok: true if it is from this story's era and place (or shows the real subject), false for a look-alike
  from another culture/era, a namesake, or something unrelated;
- sharp: 1 (blurry, tiny, damaged), 2 (usable), 3 (crisp and striking);
- hook: true only if it could open the Short: striking at a glance, clearly about the story, from the
  story's own time (never a modern museum display, replica, statue, reenactment, tourist or present-day
  photo of the place), and it raises a question instead of answering it: it must not show the twist or
  the whole story at once (a baboon working the signal levers tells the whole baboon-signalman story;
  a museum's inflatable tank gives away that the army was fake). Frame 1 must show the anomaly, disguise,
  or action described in the hook sentence, never a calm studio portrait taken before the event occurred.
  Best: the anomaly or disguised subject in action, or a strange moment whose explanation the viewer has to
  wait for;

## Picture picking
<!-- strange-history's original rules, moved from the code 2026-10-22 -->
It must be from the story's own time (never a modern
museum display, replica, statue, reenactment or present-day photo), ideally the main character's face showing
the hook's anomaly or disguise (never a calm studio portrait before the event), and must raise the hook's

## Directing
<!-- strange-history's original rules, moved from the code 2026-10-22 -->
- pic = the number of the real picture from that Short's "pictures" list that shows this scene best, or null
  when none does. Never the same number twice in a Short; at most 2 different frames of one film. Scene 1 gets
  the most striking fitting one ([opener] first): from the story's own time (never a modern museum,
  replica or tourist photo), ideally the main character's face, and never a picture that shows the twist
  or the whole story at once (it must raise the hook's question, not answer it). Write shot from the
  picture you chose. An evidence board's
  items may carry "pic" the same way.
- image_search = a 2-5 word query to find a real photo, screenshot, portrait, artwork, document,
  place or object for this scene (e.g. "Nintendo Ultra Hand toy", "Lord Byron portrait").
  video_search = a 2-6 word video search query to find real video footage showing what this
  scene is about: gameplay, hardware demo, speedrun moment, historical newsreel, documentary clip,
  experiment or place in motion (e.g. "Super Mario 64 Bowser throw gameplay",
  "Nintendo Ultra Hand 1966 demonstration", "Niagara Falls barrel 1901 film"). Make each scene's
  video_search specific to that scene's action or subject so different scenes pull different clips.
- Edit plan for every scene:
  shot = the director's note for this scene's visual, one sentence: framing (WIDE, MEDIUM,
  CLOSE-UP or DETAIL), exactly what is on screen, and what the viewer should notice. E.g.
  "CLOSE-UP: a player spinning Bowser by the tail in Super Mario 64 and launching him toward a spike bomb."
  or "CLOSE-UP: a 1500s physician in a long robe examining a dancer's swollen feet; he looks baffled."
  The visual editor uses it to pick the real video clip or image, so describe something real footage
  or pictures from this topic's era and niche could show.

## AI paintings
<!-- strange-history's original rules, moved from the code 2026-10-22 -->
One or two descriptive sentences, not a keyword list: the subject first with era-accurate details,
  then the setting, then the light (always name it: "low winter sun", "one oil lamp", "flat overcast daylight"),
  then a REAL hand-made medium of the story's era named with its physical marks, so it looks made by a person and
  can't pass for a real photo: "19th-century wood engraving, dense black hatching on yellowed paper";
  "hand-colored copperplate etching, fine cross-hatching, faded watercolor washes on foxed
  paper"; "1940s gouache illustration, flat matte paint, visible brush marks, coarse paper with halftone dots
  and slightly off-register colors"; "1930s silkscreen print, three flat inks on grainy paper, misaligned color
  layers"; "loose oil sketch on canvas board, thick visible brushstrokes, unfinished edges"; "charcoal drawing on
  toned paper, smudged shading". Never name what such a picture was printed in ("newspaper", "newsprint",
  "magazine", "book page", "poster", "bookplate"): the painter then draws the whole page around the picture,
  full of made-up text. Never "concept art", "digital illustration", "stylized", "cinematic", "highly
  detailed", "8k" or "vibrant" (they give the glossy AI look). The artwork fills the whole frame (never a framed
  poster, card or page with a border). Never "photograph" (a fake photo would pass
  as real evidence). Never a recognizable real person's face: show places, objects, crowds, hands,
  silhouettes or figures from behind. Never a close-up of the specific real object the story is about
  (a famous artifact, statue, building or document): the AI would invent what it looked like; show the
  setting or the people around it instead. Writing in the painting (a shop sign, a poster, a ship's name,
  a headline) is allowed when it helps tell the story: put the exact words in double quotes, at most 6,
  true to the story and its era (no invented names, dates or numbers). Without quotes the painting has no writing,
  so never paint a subject made of print without quoting its words (a newsstand, magazine rack, newspaper page,
  poster wall: the painter fills it with gibberish): quote the one cover that matters ("LIFE") or pick another subject.

## Opener
<!-- the planner stages scene 1 from this and the reviewer judges by it; add the owner's feedback here (2026-10-27) -->
Stage the story's strangest moment, the one its first line describes, as a 2-3 second animation built from free
low-poly library models (animals, objects, vehicles, food, tools, buildings, plants) and cartoon people by era
and role (pick the closest role, or man / woman / child; no faces; at most 3 people). Answer buildable false
only for what even these can't show (a crowd, a city). Frame 0 must already show the subject and the
threat (the raised tool, the drop, the wave); the first beat starts by 1.0 s.
Accepted by the owner (never a problem):
- Tools are swung by an unseen hand just off-screen; the handle may run off the edge of the frame.
- An animal is cut standing where it is (on a block or stump); it isn't laid down first.
- A blade stops at the first thing it isn't cutting (it cuts the neck and stops at the body).
- Low-poly cartoon models, simple colours, no gore.
Staging lessons from the live runs:
- Hop off and walk in the same direction, away from the support; a walk back toward it just stops at it.
- The camera follows the subject by itself once it moves.
- Show an animal from its side (owner, 2026-10-27: "why can't it be the side of the chicken instead of the front"):
  camera.angle "side" and the animal facing "left" or "right". Facings and directions are as seen in the shot.
- Name models plainly, the way a free low-poly library titles them: "truck", "house", "rocks", "tank",
  not "ww2 armored car" or "ruined brick house" (the first people run lost its opener to names no library has).
- Name a prop as it is held: "closed umbrella", "walking stick", "sword" (an "umbrella" came back open).
- To go at someone or something, use "toward": its id ("toward": "car"); you can't know which side it stands on.
- Two things that move toward each other (a person and a car) start far enough apart to stop short; a person
  can't stop a car, so the car stops, or it drives across the frame, not into them.
- A person's prop (the umbrella, the sword) is in frame 0; people are seen from the side like animals.
- Walks and hops go across the frame ("left" or "right"), not "away" or "camera": walking away shows its back.
- A piece that flies off flies the way the animal faces; the body then hops and walks the other way, or it
  walks over its own head.
- Chops through a neck or branch use stroke angle "diagonal" (the camera sees the blade's flat side).
- Keep the subject in front of everything else from the camera's side; nothing may hide it.
