## Topic picking
<!-- adapted from strange-history: engineering failures and near-misses told through the person who saw it coming; bridges only while the 3D kit draws only bridges; death, politics and myth rules added -->
Viewers decide in one second whether to swipe. Pick stories whose single most surprising fact fits in 8 words or fewer that anyone understands at once, with no engineering background (e.g. "One half-inch plate dropped a whole bridge.", "The bridge twisted itself apart in steady wind."). The story needs three things:
- a moment where something started to go wrong, which the Short can open inside of
- a clear how-it-broke moment it can show
- one tiny cause or twist it can save for the very end
The best sources for these stories:
- one small part that brought down something huge (a plate, a pin, a link, a weld, a clamp)
- a warning someone gave that nobody acted on in time
- a near-miss: a structure that almost failed, and the person who caught it
- a design change or shortcut that looked harmless on paper
- a force nobody planned for (wind, a marching or walking crowd, ice, a river eating away the pier)
- a famous failure whose real cause differs from the story people tell
Every Short needs a MAIN CHARACTER: one named person the story follows. The best one is the person who saw it coming (the inspector who sent the warning, the engineer who doubted the design, the professor filming the bridge). Next best is the designer whose choice caused it (Norman McLure at the Quebec Bridge, Clark Eldridge and Leon Moisseiff at Tacoma Narrows, Thomas Bouch at the Tay Bridge). When the story is about a structure, tell it through a person who built it, inspected it, warned about it or was on it. Choose a story with nobody to follow only when nothing better is left.
For now the 3D build kit draws only bridges (truss, girder, suspension, cable-stayed and eyebar-chain bridges), so pick only bridge stories one of those types can show. Dams, buildings, tunnels and machines come later. Mix bridge types, countries (US, UK, Canada, Australia, Europe) and eras, mostly before 2000. Every topic needs at least one real archival photo of the real bridge for the thumbnail.
Keep it safe and neutral. Favor near-misses and failures with few or no deaths. A death toll may be said once, plainly, and is never the point of the Short. Never use victims' names, bodies, injuries or grief. Nothing political: no blaming parties, governments or living people, and no current debates.
Engineering has its own myths (Tacoma Narrows "was resonance", engineers' iron rings were made from Quebec Bridge steel). Pick only what investigation reports, engineering papers or solid histories confirm. A famous myth can be a topic only when the Short tells the real cause, with the correction as the twist.

## Story rules
<!-- adapted from strange-history: hooks open on the moment it started to fail; one plain-word number and jargon rules added; the tiny cause saved for the last line; new Tacoma Narrows example -->
- How to tell it (adapted from the true-story Shorts that hold viewers best; follow it closely):
  hook (scene 1): viewers decide in one second whether to swipe.
  - OPEN INSIDE THE MOMENT IT STARTED TO GO WRONG, told as it happens, not as a summary: "A man walked onto a bridge that was rolling like a wave." / "The inspector measured the steel beam. It had bent overnight."
  - The hook shows WHAT is going wrong and holds back WHY, so the viewer needs the answer.
  - Weak hooks are summaries that tell the whole story at once ("The Tacoma Narrows Bridge collapsed in 1940 because of wind.", "A thin steel plate brought down a bridge in Minneapolis."). Once the viewer knows the cause, nothing is left to stay for.
  - The first sentence: at most 12 words, understood with no engineering background. Never a greeting, "did you know", a setup, a date, a bridge's full name or a person nobody knows yet.
  - Scene 1 already shows the failure, so the words add what the drawing can't: who was there, what they felt, what made it strange.
  - If the disaster is famous (Tacoma Narrows), open on its least-known moment.
  - Tell it through the main_character ("He...", "This inspector...", then their name once they act).
  - Draft 3 first sentences in hook_options, each from a different hook family (in-the-moment scene, result first, stakes, contrarian claim, before/after), then open scene 1 with the strongest.
  ONE STORY, NOT A LIST OF FACTS: link every beat to the one before with "but", "so" or "you see" (a complication or a consequence), never "and then". If the beats could be put in another order without breaking anything, it is a list: rewrite it. Never a "top 5 disasters" Short.
  scene 2 deepens the mystery or promises something bigger ("But he wasn't there to cross it. He was there to film it."). Never a date, a death toll or a recap.
  background:
  - Give background only as the answer to a question the viewer already has. Bring it in with "you see" right when it explains something ("You see, the deck sat on steel girders only eight feet deep...").
  - Never tell background as a timeline ("Construction began in 1938...", "The bridge cost six million dollars.").
  - No line starts with a date. Use a year only when it matters, in the middle of a sentence.
  - Name only the people who act in the story. Say the structure's full name once, after the hook.
  the cause: explain how it broke in plain words, the way you'd explain it to a friend on a napkin.
  - Use ONE concrete number the viewer can picture ("girders only eight feet deep", "a plate half an inch thick", "a crack a tenth of an inch deep").
  - Turn it into something everyday when that helps ("thinner than your thumb").
  - One number carries the Short. Add a second only when it is the twist. Use the units the builders used.
  - Engineering words (gusset plate, eyebar, fatigue, scour, flutter, cantilever) get a plain explanation in the same line ("a gusset plate, the steel sheet that ties the beams together"), or are swapped for plain words.
  escalate: each beat bigger or closer to the break than the last.
  - Use concrete, physical details: what people saw, heard and felt, the sound of the steel, the load, the wind, the measurement.
  - Tell it fast, like an engineer friend at a bar, with one or two dry, wry asides about a design or a bad idea, never about the people hurt.
  - A death toll, when the story needs one, is said once, plainly, and never in the hook or the last line.
  payoff LAST: the tiny cause or the twist is the final scene or the final two, and the Short ends there. No epilogue after it, no lessons learned, and no "follow for more" (an on-screen subscribe button and the pinned comment handle that). Where it fits, the reveal echoes the hook so the ending loops into a rewatch.
  Example of the style (a story for illustration only; never reuse its wording), one picture per scene:
  ["A man walked onto a bridge that was rolling like a wave.", "But he wasn't there to cross it. He was there to film it.",
  "You see, Professor Farquharson had been hired to calm this bridge down,", "because it had bounced since the day it opened.",
  "Drivers came just to ride the bumps, so locals named it Galloping Gertie.", "But that morning, the bouncing turned into a twist.",
  "One edge of the road rose while the other dropped,", "so the deck looked like a towel being wrung out.",
  "You see, the deck sat on steel girders only eight feet deep,", "so thin and flat that every twist let the wind push it harder.",
  "Farquharson kept filming as each swing grew bigger than the last,", "until the middle of the bridge tore loose and dropped into the water.",
  "But the twist itself started at one tiny part:", "a clamp in the middle of the cable that slid loose."]

## Writing for the picture
<!-- adapted from strange-history: every scene is drawn in 3D, so lines point at the bridge's parts and motion; real photos are research and the thumbnail -->
- Write for the drawing: every scene on this channel is drawn in 3D (Blender, an ink technical-manual look), and only the thumbnail is a real photo. So every scene must be about something the drawing of the bridge can show:
  - the whole bridge in its river, valley or bay
  - its towers, piers, deck, cables or truss
  - one plate, beam, pin, rivet, bolt or weld
  - small cars, a train or tiny people on it for scale
  - the part bending, cracking, slipping or swaying, the span falling, and what was left
  Never write an abstract line like "engineering changed forever". If a line has nothing to show, rewrite it around a concrete part or what the bridge was doing.
- Faces, documents, rooms and crowds can't be drawn. Keep lines about people (a warning, a telegram, a meeting) short and few, and tie each one to what the bridge was doing at that moment ("He sent a telegram: stop loading the bridge. But the crews kept working." while the bridge is still being built).
- Follow the drawing's order:
  - scene 1 shows the failure close up
  - then the story starts over with the whole bridge
  - the bridge is built during the background lines
  - the camera pushes in to the part when the narration first points at it
  - the break lands on the how-it-broke line
  - the camera pulls back to the result
  So point at the failing part in words before the break, and say the how-it-broke moment in one line the break can land on.
- Build on the real pictures: each topic's "pictures" lists the real photos and film found for it (#number, what it shows; [opener] = strong enough to open the Short). They are research for the story and the source of the thumbnail. An [opener] becomes the thumbnail, so when one fits, write the hook about the moment that photo shows. The story rules above still come first: never bend a fact or the pacing to fit a picture.

## Lessons from our numbers
<!-- adapted from gaming: engineering comparisons, the drawn look and the thumbnail photo added -->
A new channel: we have no numbers of our own yet, and the drawn 3D look is new for us (our other channels use real photos). Until we have numbers, watch the weak spots our strange-history channel found, and check these after every 10 Shorts:
- The first second: how many viewers swipe away (in the Shorts feed, "viewed vs. swiped away"). Strange-history lost 4-8 of every 10 viewers here even on Shorts the feed pushed. Aim to keep more than half, and see whether a drawn failure preview holds viewers as well as a real photo did.
- The 4-12 second dip: strange-history's retention curves dropped hardest here. A scene 2 that doesn't deepen the mystery, or background that starts too early, loses viewers at this point.
- The end: whether the curve stays flat until the last scene. Strange-history's two best Shorts held about 90% of the video because the main question stayed open until the end. A last line that lands should also show up as rewatches (above 100% viewed) and comments arguing about the cause.
Also compare which kinds of Shorts bring subscribers, not only views:
- famous disasters (Tacoma Narrows) vs. little-known ones
- collapses vs. near-misses
- bridge types: suspension vs. truss vs. girder vs. eyebar chain
- US bridges vs. UK, Canadian, Australian and other bridges
- a named person who saw it coming vs. none
- the thumbnail photo's click-through in search and browse, since it is the only real picture
Once 20 or more Shorts are out, replace this section with what our own numbers say.

## Opening picture
<!-- adapted from strange-history: era_ok judges the exact structure (not its replacement or a sister bridge); no death or injury; the opener is also the thumbnail; engineering examples -->
- era_ok: true if it shows this exact structure at the time of the story (or the real people, drawings and documents of its design, building or investigation). False for:
  - a different bridge of the same type, or a sister bridge built to the same design
  - the replacement built later on the same site (the 1950 Tacoma Narrows Bridge, the second Tay Bridge, the finished 1917 Quebec Bridge when the story is the 1907 collapse)
  - a present-day photo of the site, a memorial, a plaque, a museum model or a computer render
  - a namesake, or something unrelated;
- sharp: 1 (blurry, tiny, damaged), 2 (usable), 3 (crisp and striking);
- hook: true only if it could open the Short; it also becomes the thumbnail. It must:
  - be striking at a glance and clearly about the story: the structure caught failing (twisting, sagging, broken off), or whole and huge just before, ideally with tiny people or vehicles for scale;
  - come from the story's own time. Never a present-day photo, a memorial, a replica, a museum display, a documentary graphic, or arrows, circles or text someone else added;
  - never show death or injury: no bodies, victims, stretchers, rescues of the hurt or grieving families;
  - raise a question instead of answering it. It must not show the tiny cause or the whole story at once: a lab close-up of the cracked eyebar tells the whole Silver Bridge story, and an investigator's photo of the torn gusset plate gives away the reveal.
  Best: the structure in the moment it starts to fail, or the main character with the structure;

## Picture picking
<!-- adapted from strange-history: the exact structure from its own time, no death or injury, engineering examples -->
It must be a real photo or film frame of this exact structure from the story's own time (never the replacement bridge, a sister bridge, a present-day photo of the site, a memorial, a museum model or a render). Ideally it shows the structure in the moment it starts to fail, or whole and huge with the main character on or near it. It never shows death or injury. It must raise the hook's question, not answer it: never a picture that shows the tiny cause or the whole story at once (a lab close-up of the cracked eyebar tells the whole Silver Bridge story; an investigator's photo of the torn gusset plate gives away the reveal). ## Directing
<!-- adapted from strange-history: shots describe the 3D drawing; searches for archival bridge photos and film; no victims -->
- On this channel every scene is drawn in 3D and only the thumbnail is a real photo. Still fill pic, image_search, video_search and shot for every scene: the real photos are the thumbnail, the research and the fact check, and the shots tell the review what each drawn scene should show.
- pic = the number of the real picture from that Short's "pictures" list that shows this scene best, or null when none does.
  - Never use the same number twice in a Short, and use at most 2 different frames of one film.
  - Scene 1 gets the most striking picture that fits ([opener] first). It must be a real photo or film frame of this exact structure from the story's own time (never the replacement bridge, a present-day photo of the site, a memorial, a museum model or a render), ideally the structure in the moment it fails or the main character with it.
  - Scene 1's picture never shows death or injury, and never the tiny cause: it raises the hook's question, it doesn't answer it.
- image_search = a 2-5 word query to find a real archival photo, engineering drawing, newspaper page, investigation photo or portrait for this scene (e.g. "Tacoma Narrows Bridge 1940", "Quebec Bridge 1907 construction", "Silver Bridge Point Pleasant 1928", "Farquharson Tacoma Narrows filming").
  video_search = a 2-6 word query to find real footage of what this scene is about: the failure on film, a newsreel, construction footage, a model or wind-tunnel test, or the bridge in use (e.g. "Tacoma Narrows Bridge collapse film", "Galloping Gertie 1940 newsreel", "Quebec Bridge 1917 span lift").
  - Name the exact bridge and the moment, never just the type ("suspension bridge collapse"), so different scenes pull different results and nobody mixes up a sister bridge or the replacement.
  - Never search for victims, bodies or rescues.
- Edit plan for every scene:
  shot = the director's note for this scene's drawing, in one sentence: the framing (WIDE, MEDIUM, CLOSE-UP or DETAIL), exactly which part of the bridge is on screen and what it is doing, and what the viewer should notice. E.g.:
  - "WIDE: the whole suspension bridge across the narrows, its thin flat deck rising and falling in long waves; notice how shallow the deck is."
  - "DETAIL: the steel clamp where the hangers meet the main cable at mid-span; notice it has slid along the cable."
  - "CLOSE-UP: one riveted plate joining the beams under the deck, bowing outward as the train crosses."
  Name the same part the narration names. Describe only what a clean ink drawing of the bridge, its parts, small vehicles and tiny figures could show (no faces, rooms or documents).

## AI paintings
<!-- adapted from strange-history: engineering-era subjects and mediums; no close-ups of the real structure or failed part, nobody hurt -->
Write one or two descriptive sentences, not a keyword list, in this order:
1. The subject, with details true to its era and trade: the tools, clothes and materials of the right year (a riveting gang heating rivets in a coal forge on a 1900s steel truss, a 1930s drafting room with slide rules and linen drawings pinned to the boards, an inspector with an oil lantern under a cast-iron girder).
2. The setting (the river gorge, the windy strait, the railway embankment, the site office).
3. The light. Always name it: "low winter sun", "one oil lamp", "flat overcast daylight", "storm light before rain".
4. A REAL hand-made medium of the story's era, named with its physical marks, so it looks made by a person and can't pass for a real photo: "19th-century wood engraving from an illustrated newspaper, dense black hatching on yellowed newsprint"; "pen-and-ink drawing on drafting linen, fine ruled lines, cross-hatched shadows"; "1940s gouache magazine illustration, flat matte paint, visible brush marks, coarse paper with halftone dots"; "1930s silkscreen poster, three flat inks on grainy paper, misaligned color layers"; "loose oil sketch on canvas board, thick visible brushstrokes, unfinished edges"; "charcoal drawing on toned paper, smudged shading".
Never write:
- "concept art", "digital illustration", "3D render", "CAD", "blueprint render", "stylized", "cinematic", "highly detailed", "8k" or "vibrant". They give the glossy AI look.
- "photograph": a fake photo would pass as real evidence.
The artwork fills the whole frame: never a framed poster, card or page with a border.
What not to show:
- A recognizable real person's face. Show the structure from afar, workers from behind, hands, tools, silhouettes or crowds instead.
- A close-up of the specific real structure or part the story is about (the Tacoma Narrows deck, the Silver Bridge eyebar, the I-35W gusset plate). The AI would invent what it looked like, and the engineers in the audience notice a wrong truss or a floating span. Real photos and our 3D drawing show those. Show the people, tools, weather and setting around it instead, with any bridge small and far away.
- Anyone falling, hurt, trapped or dead, wreckage with people in it, rescues or grief.
Writing in the painting (a telegram, a site sign, a newspaper headline, a warning notice) is allowed when it helps tell the story:
- Put the exact words in double quotes, at most 6, true to the story and its era (no invented names, dates or numbers), e.g. Theodore Cooper's telegram "ADD NO MORE LOAD TO BRIDGE".
- Without quotes, the painting has no writing. So never paint a subject made of print without quoting its words (a drawing sheet, a blueprint, a newspaper page, a notice board): the painter fills it with gibberish. Quote the one line that matters or pick another subject.

## Build plan
<!-- new -->
You draw every scene of the Short in Blender: one bridge, one failing part in red, one failure. Plan it so a viewer who never studied engineering sees what the bridge was, where it broke and how.
Structure (match the real bridge from the research):
- template: the real bridge's type:
  - truss_bridge: truss and cantilever-truss bridges (Quebec Bridge, Tay Bridge, Ashtabula, I-35W)
  - girder_bridge: beam, plate-girder and box-girder bridges (Dee Bridge, Kings Bridge, Mianus River, Schoharie Creek)
  - suspension_bridge: Tacoma Narrows, Millennium Bridge
  - cable_stayed_bridge: West Gate
  - eyebar_chain_bridge: bridges hung from chains of flat steel links (Silver Bridge)
  When the bridge fits none exactly, use the closest look.
- truss: the real pattern when the research names it (howe for Ashtabula's iron Howe truss, warren for I-35W), else warren.
- spans, span_m, height_m, width_m: the real numbers from the research, rounded. The proportions matter more than precision. When a number is missing, pick one that is believable for the type and era.
- deck: rail for railway bridges (Tay, Dee, Ashtabula, Quebec), road otherwise.
- setting: river, valley (a gorge or dry valley) or bay (wide water or a strait, like the Tacoma Narrows).
- fastener: what really held the bridge together, by era:
  - pin: eyebar chains and pin-and-hanger joints
  - rivet: most iron and steel bridges before about 1960
  - bolt: later bridges and repairs
  - weld: welded girders from the 1950s on (Kings Bridge)
  Never screws.
Focus: the one part the story is about, where it really was.
- part: the closest from the list: eyebar (Silver Bridge), gusset_plate (I-35W), chord (Quebec's bowed bottom beam), pin_joint (Mianus River), cable (Tacoma's mid-span cable clamp), pier (Schoharie Creek, Tay). Use fastener_group when the story is about the rivets or bolts themselves.
- at: which span, panel, top or bottom chord and side it was on. A part outside the truss (cable, hanger, eyebar, pier, bearing) still gets its span and side.
- label: the part's name as a technical manual would print it, upper case, 40 characters at most ("GUSSET PLATE U10", "EYEBAR 330", "BOTTOM CHORD A9L"). The red part and its label show in scene 1's preview and again from the first zoom, so the label must never give away the twist: no flaw, thickness or number unless the hook already says it. When even the part's name is the twist (Tacoma's slipping clamp), label is null.
Failure: how the focus part really failed.
- kind:
  - crack: a crack grows (fatigue, a hidden flaw, brittle steel, a weld)
  - bend: a beam or plate buckles (overload, too thin, too slender)
  - shear: rivets, bolts or a pin let go and the joint slips
  - sway: the deck sways or twists (wind, marching or walking crowds)
- then: collapse_span only when that span really fell. Use none for near-misses, failures caught in time and bridges that stood (Millennium Bridge). Never invent a collapse.
- span: the span that fell.
Scale: what was really on the bridge, so the viewer feels its size: car, train, people. People are always tiny and never shown falling or hurt. Leave people out of any Short with collapse_span, and out of any Short whose failure killed someone.
Scenes: one beat per scene, read from the narration:
- failure_preview: scene 1, always. The finished bridge, close on the red part as it fails; then the story starts over.
- wide: the whole bridge, calm, when the narration first brings it in (usually scene 2).
- build: the background lines about how it was designed and built ("You see..."). The parts drop in over all the build scenes, so give build to 2-4 scenes in a row, before any zoom.
- orbit: a slow turn around the finished bridge, for lines about it in use, its quirks or the warning. Use one or two in a row.
- zoom: the push in to the red part, with its fasteners and label.
  - The first zoom goes where the narration first points at the trouble.
  - A later zoom pushes back in on the broken part, best for the final reveal.
- failure: the how-it-broke line. The failure plays once, on the first failure scene, peaking on that line's emphasis word. A second failure scene holds close on the break, so put the first failure on the line where it actually breaks, not on earlier lines about it moving.
- aftermath: a pull back from the part to the whole result, for the lines right after the break.
There must be a zoom before the first failure (otherwise the last third of the scenes is replaced by zoom, failure, aftermath). The last scene is usually aftermath, or zoom when the reveal names the part.

 "focus": {"part": "cable", "at": {"span": 1, "side": "left"}, "label": null},
 "failure": {"kind": "sway", "then": "collapse_span", "span": 1}, "scale": ["car"]}
beats by scene: 1 failure_preview (the man walks onto the rolling bridge), 2 wide, 3-4 build (hired to calm it, bouncing since it opened), 5 orbit (Galloping Gertie), 6-8 zoom (the bouncing turns into a twist), 9-10 orbit (the shallow girders), 11 zoom (each swing bigger), 12 failure (the middle tears loose), 13 aftermath (one tiny part), 14 zoom (the clamp that slid loose).
