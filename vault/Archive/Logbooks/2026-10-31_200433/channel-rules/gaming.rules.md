## Topic picking
<!-- adapted from strange-history: main character can be a developer, player or game character; gaming topic sources, franchise mix and a fact-check rule added -->
Viewers decide in one second whether to swipe. Pick stories whose single most surprising fact fits in 8 words or fewer that any gamer understands instantly (e.g. "Mario's mustache exists because pixels were too big.", "Pokémon's rarest monster was a last-minute secret."). The story needs a strange moment the Short can open inside of, and an answer or twist it can save for the very end.
The best sources for these stories:
- hidden secrets and easter eggs
- cut content and prototypes
- bugs that became features
- hardware limits that forced a famous design
- record-breaking or speedrun moments
- lore that changes how a famous scene reads
Every Short needs a MAIN CHARACTER: one named developer, speedrunner, player or game character the story follows (Tomohiro Nishikado, Shigeru Miyamoto, a world-record speedrunner, Master Chief, Ganondorf). There must be real footage, screenshots or photos of them. When the story is about a thing (a glitch, a cut level, a console), tell it through the person who made it, found it or broke it: Mew in Pokémon through Shigeki Morimoto, who slipped it in at the last minute, or the Space Invaders speed-up through Nishikado. Choose a story with nobody to follow only when nothing better is left.
Favor franchises the audience already plays (Zelda, Mario, Pokémon, Halo, Elden Ring, GTA, Minecraft, Final Fantasy, Metal Gear), and mix eras: retro, 2000s and recent hits.
Gaming is full of fake facts. Pick only what developers, interviews, datamines or published records confirm. A famous rumor (Polybius, Mew under the truck) can be a topic only when the Short tells the truth behind it, with the debunk as the twist.

## Story rules
<!-- adapted from strange-history: hooks, examples and background lines rewritten for games; jargon and list-Short bans added; new Space Invaders example -->
- How to tell it (adapted from the true-story Shorts that hold viewers best; follow it closely):
  hook (scene 1): viewers decide in one second whether to swipe.
  - OPEN INSIDE THE STORY'S STRANGEST MOMENT, told as it happens, not as a summary: "The last invader on the screen suddenly started racing toward you." / "Mario jumped into a wall and came out in a level that doesn't exist."
  - The hook shows WHAT is happening and holds back WHY, so the viewer needs the answer.
  - Weak hooks are summaries that tell the whole story at once ("Space Invaders sped up because of a hardware bug.", "Mew was added to Pokémon at the last minute."). Once the viewer knows the story, nothing is left to stay for.
  - The first sentence: at most 12 words, and any gamer understands it with no context. Never a greeting, "did you know", a setup, a release year or a name nobody knows yet.
  - The opening picture already shows the game, so the words add what it can't.
  - If the story is famous (Missingno., the Konami Code), open on its least-known moment.
  - Tell it through the main_character ("He...", "This player...", then their name once they act).
  - Draft 3 first sentences in hook_options, each from a different hook family (in-the-moment scene, result first, stakes, contrarian claim, before/after), then open scene 1 with the strongest.
  ONE STORY, NOT A LIST OF FACTS: link every beat to the one before with "but", "so" or "you see" (a complication or a consequence), never "and then". If the beats could be put in another order without breaking anything, it is a list: rewrite it. Never a "top 5" or "3 secrets in..." Short.
  scene 2 deepens the mystery or promises something bigger ("But nobody had ever programmed it to speed up."). Never a release date or a recap.
  background:
  - Give background only as the answer to a question the viewer already has. Bring it in with "you see" right when it explains something ("You see, the chip could barely draw 55 aliens at once...").
  - Never tell background as a timeline ("In 1978, Taito released...", "The game sold 20 million copies.").
  - No line starts with a date. Use a year only when it matters, in the middle of a sentence.
  - Name only the people who act in the story.
  - Say a game's full name the first time ("Ocarina of Time", not "OoT").
  - No speedrun or modding jargon ("clip", "OOB", "RNG manip", "frame-perfect") without a plain-word explanation in the same line.
  escalate: each beat stranger or bigger than the last. Use concrete, specific details (what was on screen, the button pressed, the frame count, the memory limit, what the developer said). Tell it fast, like a friend who knows every secret, with one or two quick jokes or wry asides that make it fun. Never mock players, developers or a game's fans.
  payoff LAST: the answer to the hook's question (the reveal or twist) is the final scene or the final two, and the Short ends there. No epilogue after it, and no "follow for more" (an on-screen subscribe button and the pinned comment handle that). Where it fits, the reveal echoes the hook so the ending loops into a rewatch.
  Example of the style (a story for illustration only; never reuse its wording), one picture per scene:
  ["The last invader on the screen suddenly started racing toward you.", "Players panicked and fed the machine coin after coin.",
  "But nobody had ever programmed it to speed up.", "You see, Tomohiro Nishikado built Space Invaders almost alone,",
  "even the hardware, and his chip could barely draw 55 aliens.", "So with a full screen, the whole army crawled.",
  "Every alien you shot gave the chip one less to draw,", "so the rest marched a little faster.",
  "Nishikado spotted the problem. He could have fixed it.", "But players were sweating, leaning in, begging for one more try.",
  "So he kept the bug, and it became one of gaming's first difficulty curves.",
  "That last alien was never coded to be fast.", "It finally had the whole chip to itself."]

## Writing for the picture
<!-- adapted from strange-history: the things a scene can show are now gameplay, screenshots, hardware and dev photos; real pictures include footage -->
- Write for the picture: every scene must be about something real footage or a picture could show, such as an in-game moment, a character, a level, a menu or glitch, a console or cabinet, a developer, a box, a magazine page or a prototype screenshot. Never write an abstract line like "this changed gaming forever". If a line has nothing to show, rewrite it around a concrete detail (the screen, the button, the sprite, the room).
- Build on the real pictures: each topic's "pictures" lists the real screenshots, photos and gameplay footage found for it (#number, what it shows; [opener] = strong enough to open the Short). Where a strong picture exists, let the story pass through what it shows, and write the hook for an [opener] picture when one fits. The story rules above still come first: never bend a fact or the pacing to fit a picture.

## Opening picture
<!-- adapted from strange-history: era_ok judges the right game, version and platform; hook bans thumbnails, facecams, overlays and fan art; gaming examples -->
- era_ok: true if it is from the right game, version and platform for this story (or shows the real developer, player or hardware). False for:
  - a different game or a sequel
  - a remaster or remake when the story is about the original, or the other way round
  - a fan mod, fan art or fan remake
  - a namesake, or something unrelated;
- sharp: 1 (blurry, tiny, heavily compressed, a phone filming a screen), 2 (usable), 3 (crisp and striking);
- hook: true only if it could open the Short. It must:
  - be striking at a glance and clearly about the story;
  - come from the game itself or its real making. Never a YouTuber's thumbnail, a facecam, a watermark, a meme, fan art, a modern merch shot, or text or arrows someone else added;
  - raise a question instead of answering it. It must not show the twist or the whole story at once: a screenshot of Mew's sprite in its hidden spot tells the whole secret, and a side-by-side of the cut and final versions gives away the reveal.
  Best: the main character's face, or a strange in-game moment whose explanation the viewer has to wait for;

## Directing
<!-- adapted from strange-history: picture bans and search query examples changed to games; no-commentary footage preferred; gaming shot example -->
- pic = the number of the real picture from that Short's "pictures" list that shows this scene best, or null when none does.
  - Never use the same number twice in a Short, and use at most 2 different frames of one clip.
  - Scene 1 gets the most striking picture that fits ([opener] first). It must come from the game itself or its real making (never fan art, a YouTuber's thumbnail, a watermark or added text), ideally the main character or the strange moment. It must never show the twist or the whole story at once: it raises the hook's question, it doesn't answer it.
  - Write shot from the picture you chose.
  - An evidence board's items may carry "pic" the same way.
- image_search = a 2-5 word query to find a real screenshot, box art, developer photo, magazine scan, prototype, hardware or official concept art for this scene (e.g. "Space Invaders arcade cabinet", "Tomohiro Nishikado portrait", "Ocarina of Time beta screenshot").
  video_search = a 2-6 word query to find real footage of what this scene is about: gameplay, a speedrun moment, a glitch, a hardware teardown, a developer interview, an old commercial or a trade-show demo (e.g. "Super Mario 64 Bowser throw gameplay", "Space Invaders 1978 arcade gameplay", "Pokemon Red Missingno glitch").
  - Name the exact game and the moment, never just the franchise ("Halo"), so different scenes pull different clips.
  - Prefer clean gameplay: add "no commentary" when a moment is usually streamed with a facecam.
- Edit plan for every scene:
  shot = the director's note for this scene's visual, in one sentence: the framing (WIDE, MEDIUM, CLOSE-UP or DETAIL), exactly what is on screen, and what the viewer should notice. E.g. "CLOSE-UP: a player spinning Bowser by the tail in Super Mario 64 and launching him toward a spike bomb." or "WIDE: the full Space Invaders formation creeping down a black screen; notice how slowly it moves." The visual editor uses it to pick the real clip or image, so describe something that real gameplay, screenshots or photos of this game and its making could show.

## AI paintings
<!-- adapted from strange-history: gaming-era subjects, light and hand-made media; pixel art, renders, fake screenshots and game characters or logos banned -->
Write one or two descriptive sentences, not a keyword list, in this order:
1. The subject, with details true to its era: the hardware, desks, clothes and screens of the right year (a 1978 Tokyo arcade, a 1990s Kyoto office with CRT monitors and graph-paper level maps).
2. The setting.
3. The light. Always name it: "blue CRT glow in a dark room", "flat fluorescent office light", "low evening sun through window blinds".
4. A REAL hand-made medium, named with its physical marks, so it looks made by a person and can't pass for a real photo or a real screenshot: "1980s airbrushed video game box art, soft gradients, visible overspray on illustration board"; "1990s Japanese game magazine ink illustration, clean brush lines, screentone dots"; "marker rendering on layout paper, streaky alcohol-marker strokes, white gouache highlights"; "gouache painting on coarse paper, flat matte paint, visible brush marks"; "loose oil sketch on canvas board, thick visible brushstrokes, unfinished edges"; "charcoal drawing on toned paper, smudged shading".
Never write:
- "concept art", "digital illustration", "3D render", "pixel art", "in-game screenshot", "Unreal Engine", "stylized", "cinematic", "highly detailed", "8k" or "vibrant". They give the glossy AI look, and pixel art or a render could pass as real cut content.
- "photograph": a fake photo would pass as real evidence.
The artwork fills the whole frame: never a framed poster, card, cartridge label or page with a border.
What not to show:
- A recognizable real person's face. Show rooms, hardware, crowds around a cabinet, hands on a controller, silhouettes or figures from behind instead.
- A recognizable game character, logo, or the specific console, cartridge or prototype the story is about, up close (Mario, Master Chief, the Nintendo PlayStation). The AI would invent what it looked like, and real footage exists for those. Show the people, rooms and screen glow around it instead.
Writing in the painting (an arcade marquee, a shop sign, a magazine cover, a sticky note on a dev's monitor) is allowed when it helps tell the story:
- Put the exact words in double quotes, at most 6, true to the story and its era (no invented names, dates or numbers).
- Without quotes, the painting has no writing. So never paint a subject made of print without quoting its words (a game shop shelf, a magazine rack, a wall of box art, a strategy guide page): the painter fills it with gibberish. Quote the one cover that matters ("NINTENDO POWER") or pick another subject.
